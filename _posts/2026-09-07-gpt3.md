## One-line summary

Train the GPT-2 architecture at 175 billion parameters on about 300 billion tokens, then evaluate by putting the task description and a few examples *in the prompt* and reading the continuation. With no fine-tuning, the model is competitive with fine-tuned systems on many benchmarks, and its few-shot ability grows smoothly with size.

## Why it matters

[GPT-2](/blog/gpt2/) showed zero-shot task ability emerging; GPT-3 shows that at sufficient scale a model can pick up a task from a handful of demonstrations written into its context, without any parameter update. "Prompting" replaces fine-tuning as the way to use a language model, which is the interface of every LLM product since.

## In-context learning, defined

Figure 2.1 fixes the vocabulary:

- **Fine-tuning:** update the weights on thousands of labeled examples. Not used for GPT-3.
- **Zero-shot:** the prompt is a natural-language description of the task (`Translate English to French: cheese =>`).
- **One-shot:** the description plus one demonstration (`sea otter => loutre de mer`).
- **Few-shot:** the description plus $$K$$ demonstrations, typically 10–100, as many as fit in the 2048-token context.

The distinction is that the demonstrations are *conditioning*, not training. The paper also frames pretraining as an *outer* loop that learns a broad set of skills and in-context learning as an *inner* loop that recognizes which skill the prompt is asking for (Figure 1.1).

## Model and training

Same decoder-only Transformer as GPT-2 (pre-norm, learned positions, byte-level BPE), with one change: attention layers alternate between dense and *locally banded sparse* patterns, as in the Sparse Transformer, to save compute. Eight sizes are trained (Table 2.1) so that scaling can be plotted; the largest:

| model | parameters | layers | $$d_{\text{model}}$$ | heads | batch | learning rate |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| GPT-3 175B | 175.0B | 96 | 12288 | 96 | 3.2M tokens | $$0.6 \times 10^{-4}$$ |

All models see 300B tokens. The head size is $$d_{\text{model}}/\text{heads} = 128$$; the FFN is $$4 d_{\text{model}}$$. Adam with $$\beta_2 = 0.95$$, gradient clipping at 1.0, cosine decay to 10% over 260B tokens, linear warmup over 375M tokens, and a batch size that ramps up during training. Training used model parallelism across a V100 cluster; total compute is roughly 3,640 petaflop/s-days for the largest model (Figure 2.2 shows compute vs parameters for all models and for BERT/RoBERTa/T5).

**Data (Table 2.2).** A filtered Common Crawl (410B tokens, classifier-filtered for similarity to high-quality references and fuzzily deduplicated), WebText2 (19B), Books1 (12B), Books2 (55B), Wikipedia (3B). The mixture is *not* proportional to size: higher-quality sources are sampled more often, so Wikipedia is seen 3.4 times over training while Common Crawl is seen 0.44 times. A test-set contamination filter removed overlaps where possible; Section 4 measures the residual effect benchmark by benchmark.

## Scaling behaviour

Figure 3.1 plots validation loss against training compute for all eight models. The lower envelope follows a power law,

$$
L(C) \approx \Big(\frac{C_c}{C}\Big)^{0.050}
$$

extending the Kaplan et al. (2020) scaling-law fit by two orders of magnitude with almost no deviation. Figure 1.3 aggregates 42 accuracy-based benchmarks: zero-, one- and few-shot curves all rise with parameters, and the *gap* between few-shot and zero-shot widens, meaning larger models are better at using demonstrations. Figure 1.2 shows the same on a synthetic task (removing random symbols from words): the few-shot curve for 175B climbs steeply with $$K$$, small models barely move.

## Results by task family

- **Language modeling and completion (3.1):** PTB perplexity 20.5 (from 35.8); LAMBADA 76.2% zero-shot and **86.4%** few-shot (from 68.0%), helped by a fill-in-the-blank style prompt; StoryCloze 87.7%; HellaSwag 79.3%.
- **Closed-book QA (3.2):** TriviaQA 71.2% few-shot, above a fine-tuned T5-11B with retrieval-free setup; Natural Questions 29.9%, WebQuestions 41.5%.
- **Translation (3.3):** few-shot into English is strong (Fr→En 39.2, De→En 40.6, Ro→En 39.5 BLEU, above prior unsupervised NMT), out of English is weaker because the model is 93% English.
- **Winograd / commonsense (3.4):** Winogrande 77.7% few-shot; PIQA 82.8% (with contamination caveats); ARC Challenge 51.5%.
- **Reading comprehension (3.5):** CoQA 85.0 F1 few-shot; DROP and QuAC are weak; RACE mid-pack.
- **SuperGLUE (3.6):** 71.8 few-shot average with $$K = 32$$, comparable to fine-tuned BERT-large, poor on WiC and on tasks comparing two sentences.
- **NLI (3.7):** near chance on RTE and ANLI, one of the clearest failures.
- **Synthetic and qualitative (3.9):** 2-digit addition 100%, 3-digit 80.4% few-shot, 4-digit much lower; word unscrambling; SAT analogies 65.2%; news-article generation where humans identify GPT-3 articles as machine-written only 52% of the time (Table 3.11, essentially chance); using novel words in a sentence; grammar correction.

## Limitations the paper states

Repetition and loss of coherence in long text; weak on tasks that compare two sentences (NLI, WiC); the pretraining objective weights every token equally and has no notion of what is useful; no grounding; poor sample efficiency compared with humans; unclear whether few-shot actually learns new tasks or retrieves ones seen in pretraining; expensive inference; uninterpretable; and biases in the data reflected in outputs (Section 6 measures gender, race and religion associations). The energy cost of training is reported.

## Thoughts

- The paper's real object is Figure 3.1: a smooth, predictable curve that made it rational to spend the compute. Everything else follows from scale.
- Few-shot prompting is fragile (format-sensitive, order-sensitive) and the model does not know what it is being asked to do; [InstructGPT](/blog/instructgpt/) addresses exactly that gap with human feedback and turns the raw model into an assistant.
- The NLI failures and the "compare two sentences" weakness are where BERT-style encoders remain better per parameter, a distinction T5 quantified.
