## One-line summary

Make each group of layers learn a *correction* $$F(x)$$ that is added to its input, $$y = F(x) + x$$, so that very deep networks can be trained; with this, 152-layer networks beat everything on ImageNet and a 1000-layer network trains on CIFAR.

## Why it is in a language-model reading list

Every Transformer sublayer is wrapped as $$x + \text{Sublayer}(x)$$. That is the residual connection from this paper, and the reason a 96-layer GPT-3 can be optimized at all. The GRU's $$z \odot h + (1 - z) \odot \tilde h$$ and the LSTM cell were additive shortcuts *through time*; ResNet makes the shortcut *through depth* and shows it is the thing that lets depth scale.

## The degradation problem

Figure 1 is the motivation. On CIFAR-10, a plain 56-layer network has *higher training error* than a 20-layer one. This is not overfitting (training error is worse, not just test error) and not vanishing gradients in the classic sense (both networks use batch normalization and train). Deeper plain networks are simply harder to optimize, even though a deeper network could in principle copy the shallower one and add identity layers. The optimizer does not find that solution.

## Residual learning, equation by equation

Let the desired mapping of a few stacked layers be $$H(x)$$. Instead of fitting $$H$$, fit the residual $$F(x) := H(x) - x$$ and add the input back:

$$
y = F(x, \{W_i\}) + x
$$

For a two-layer block with ReLU $$\sigma$$, $$F(x) = W_2\, \sigma(W_1 x)$$, and a ReLU is applied after the addition. The shortcut adds no parameters and no compute. When the dimensions differ (at the four stage transitions where channels double and resolution halves), a linear projection is used on the shortcut,

$$
y = F(x, \{W_i\}) + W_s x
$$

but the paper shows (Table 3, options A/B/C) that projections are not essential: zero-padding the extra channels works nearly as well, so the identity form is kept everywhere it can be.

**Why this helps.** If the optimal mapping for a block is near the identity, a plain block must learn to reproduce $$x$$ through two nonlinear layers, while a residual block just has to drive $$F$$ to zero, which weight decay already encourages. More generally, the block's output is a *small perturbation* of its input, so adding blocks cannot make the function much worse than before, and the gradient of the loss with respect to $$x$$ contains a direct term $$\partial y / \partial x = I + \partial F / \partial x$$: the "$$I$$" carries gradient straight through, no matter how small $$\partial F / \partial x$$ is. Figure 7 later confirms that the learned $$F$$ responses are small: layer output standard deviations are lower in ResNets than in plain nets, and lower for deeper ResNets.

**Bottleneck block.** For the 50/101/152-layer models each block is 1×1 (reduce channels), 3×3, 1×1 (restore channels), so the expensive 3×3 convolution runs at a quarter of the width. The 152-layer ResNet has *fewer* FLOPs (11.3 billion) than VGG-19 (19.6 billion).

## Figures, explained

- **Figure 1 (degradation).** Training and test error on CIFAR-10 for 20- vs 56-layer plain networks; the deeper one is worse on both curves.
- **Figure 2 (the residual block).** Two weight layers, a ReLU between them, a shortcut arc from the input to a ⊕ node, then a ReLU. This is the diagram to keep in mind when reading "Add & Norm" in the Transformer figure.
- **Figure 3 (three 34-layer-scale networks).** Left VGG-19, middle a 34-layer plain net, right the same with shortcut arcs; dotted arcs mark dimension changes. It makes the point that ResNet is *just* plain net plus arcs.
- **Figure 4 (ImageNet training curves).** Left: 18- vs 34-layer plain nets; the 34-layer one is worse throughout. Right: the same two depths with residual connections; now 34 beats 18 by 2.8% top-1, and converges faster.
- **Figure 5 (bottleneck).** Basic block (two 3×3, 64 channels) next to the bottleneck (1×1 64 → 3×3 64 → 1×1 256).
- **Figure 6 (CIFAR-10 curves).** Plain nets get worse with depth (20 → 56 → 110 layers); ResNets get better (20 → 110); a 1202-layer ResNet trains fine but slightly overfits.
- **Figure 7 (response magnitudes).** Standard deviation of each layer's output after batch norm, sorted; ResNet layers respond less, and deeper ResNets even less, evidence that residual functions stay close to zero.

## Results

ImageNet 2012: ResNet-152 single model 4.49% top-5 error; a six-model ensemble **3.57%**, first place in ILSVRC 2015 classification, and first in detection and localization on ImageNet and COCO by using ResNet-101 as the backbone. CIFAR-10: ResNet-110 reaches 6.43% error, ResNet-1202 trains to 7.93%.

## Thoughts

- The Transformer keeps the addition and moves the normalization next to it: $$\text{LayerNorm}(x + \text{Sublayer}(x))$$ in the original, or the "pre-norm" $$x + \text{Sublayer}(\text{LayerNorm}(x))$$ that GPT-2 and most later models use because it trains more stably at depth, exactly the concern of this paper.
- "Identity is easy to represent" is the same reason GRU/LSTM gates default to copying. Skip connections are the one idea that appears in every successful deep architecture.
- The paper's follow-up ("Identity Mappings in Deep Residual Networks") formalizes the gradient argument: with pure identity shortcuts, $$x_L = x_l + \sum_{i=l}^{L-1} F(x_i)$$, so any layer's output is the input plus a *sum* of residuals, not a product of transformations.
