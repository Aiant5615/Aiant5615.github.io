## One-line summary

Run [GRPO](/blog/deepseekmath-grpo/) on a 671B base model with nothing but *rule-based* rewards (is the final answer correct? is the output in the required format?) and no supervised data, and the model teaches itself to think for thousands of tokens, re-check its work, and reach 71% pass@1 on AIME 2024 from 15.6%. Add a small cold-start dataset and three more stages to fix readability, and the resulting DeepSeek-R1 matches OpenAI's o1 on reasoning benchmarks; distilling its outputs into small dense models beats running RL on them directly.

## Why it matters

Every earlier paper in this series needed a *learned* reward model and started from an SFT model. R1-Zero shows that for tasks with checkable answers, RL with a verifier on top of a base model is enough to produce long-form reasoning, and that behaviours like reflection and "aha" moments appear without being demonstrated. It made RL-for-reasoning the dominant recipe of 2025.

## DeepSeek-R1-Zero

**Algorithm.** GRPO exactly as in DeepSeekMath: a group of $$G$$ outputs per question, group-normalized advantages $$A_i = (r_i - \operatorname{mean}(r)) / \operatorname{std}(r)$$, the clipped ratio objective, and a KL term to the reference estimated with the unbiased $$\frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}}{\pi_{\theta}} - \log \frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}}{\pi_{\theta}} - 1$$ (Figure 3 in the paper's appendix redraws PPO vs GRPO; Figure 4 shows GRPO learning faster than PPO on MATH at equal cost).

**Rewards, with no reward model.**

- *Accuracy reward:* deterministic checking of the final answer, given in a required form (a boxed answer for math, compiler tests for code).
- *Format reward:* the reasoning must be enclosed between `<think>` and `</think>` tags, followed by the answer.

The paper explicitly avoids neural reward models, including process reward models, because they get *hacked* during large-scale RL and add training complexity.

**Template (Table 1).** A short system prompt asks the assistant to "first think about the reasoning process in the mind and then provide the user with the answer", with the two tag pairs; nothing prescribes *how* to reason, so any strategy the model develops is its own.

**What happens.** Figure 1(a): AIME 2024 pass@1 climbs from 15.6% to 71.0% over training (86.7% with 64-sample majority voting, matching o1-0912). Figure 1(b): the average response length grows steadily to many thousands of tokens; the model *chooses* to think longer because it pays. Table 2 shows the "aha moment": an intermediate checkpoint writing "Wait, wait. Wait. That's an aha moment I can flag here" and re-deriving a step. Problems: the outputs mix languages and are hard to read.

## DeepSeek-R1: the four-stage pipeline (Figure 2)

1. **Cold start.** Collect a few thousand long chain-of-thought examples (few-shot prompting, R1-Zero outputs rewritten by humans into a readable format with a summary at the end) and SFT DeepSeek-V3-Base on them. Purpose: readability and a better starting point for RL.
2. **Reasoning-oriented RL.** GRPO with the rule-based rewards plus a **language-consistency reward** (fraction of target-language words in the chain of thought), which slightly lowers benchmark scores but removes language mixing; the rewards are summed.
3. **Rejection sampling and SFT.** Sample from the stage-2 checkpoint, keep correct and readable responses (about 600k reasoning samples, judged by rules and by DeepSeek-V3 for open-ended cases), add about 200k non-reasoning samples (writing, QA, translation) reusing V3's SFT pipeline, and fine-tune V3-Base on the 800k for two epochs.
4. **RL for all scenarios.** A second RL stage combining rule-based rewards for reasoning data with V3-style *preference* reward models for helpfulness (judged on the final summary) and harmlessness (judged on the whole response), over a mixed prompt distribution.

**Distillation.** Fine-tune Qwen2.5 and Llama-3 models (1.5B to 70B) on the 800k samples from stage 3 with SFT only, no RL. DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-14B beats QwQ-32B; the 32B and 70B distilled models beat o1-mini on most benchmarks.

## Results (Table 3–5)

DeepSeek-R1: AIME 2024 **79.8%** pass@1, MATH-500 97.3%, Codeforces rating 2029 (above 96% of humans), GPQA Diamond 71.5%, MMLU 90.8%, on par with o1-1217. Distilled 7B: 55.5% AIME, above GPT-4o and Claude-3.5-Sonnet at the time.

**Distillation vs RL on small models (Section 4.1).** RL directly on Qwen-32B-Base for 10k steps gives 47% AIME; distilling R1's outputs into the same model gives 72.6%. Conclusion: large-model reasoning patterns transfer through SFT more cheaply than they can be discovered by RL at small scale.

**Unsuccessful attempts (Section 4.2).** Process reward models (hard to define steps, expensive to label, reward hacking); Monte-Carlo tree search over token-level generation (search space too large, a fine-grained value model too hard to train). Both are reported as negative results at this scale, not as impossibilities.

## Thoughts

- The essential ingredients are a *verifiable* reward, a policy strong enough to sometimes get it right (so the group has non-zero variance), and enough compute for the response length to grow. Everything else in the pipeline is about making the result usable.
- "No reward model" is the sharpest contrast with the human-feedback line of this series: for math and code, the environment provides the reward, exactly as in the MuJoCo experiments of [Christiano et al.](/blog/christiano-preferences/), and the preference model is only needed for the fuzzy final stage.
- The distillation result reframes RL as a way to *discover* reasoning traces once, at scale, then spread them by SFT.
