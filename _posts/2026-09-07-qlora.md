## One-line summary

Store the frozen base model in 4 bits with a data type matched to the distribution of neural-network weights, dequantize each layer on the fly to compute activations and gradients, and route the gradients into 16-bit LoRA adapters. A 65B model fine-tunes on a single 48GB GPU with no measurable loss versus 16-bit full fine-tuning, and the resulting Guanaco models reach 99.3% of ChatGPT's score on the Vicuna benchmark after a day of training.

## Why it matters

[LoRA](/blog/lora/) shrank the *trainable* parameters but the frozen weights still had to sit in memory in 16 bits: 130GB for a 65B model, plus activations. QLoRA cut the base to a quarter and made fine-tuning of the largest open models a consumer-hardware activity; the paper also produced the (then) strongest open chatbot and a large ablation of instruction datasets.

## Block-wise quantization, restated

Quantizing a tensor $$X$$ from 32/16-bit to $$k$$-bit integers rescales by a constant $$c$$ so the range fits, e.g. for int8

$$
X^{\text{Int8}} = \operatorname{round}\Big(\frac{127}{\operatorname{absmax}(X^{\text{FP32}})} X^{\text{FP32}}\Big) = \operatorname{round}(c\, X^{\text{FP32}}), \qquad \operatorname{dequant}(c, X^{\text{Int8}}) = X^{\text{Int8}} / c
$$

A single outlier would waste most of the levels, so the tensor is cut into blocks (64 values) each with its own constant $$c$$, at the price of storing one constant per block.

## The three ingredients

**1. 4-bit NormalFloat (NF4).** Pretrained weights are approximately normally distributed with mean zero, so the *information-theoretically optimal* 4-bit code assigns one level to each equal-probability bin of a normal distribution. The levels are the midpoints between quantiles of $$N(0, 1)$$,

$$
q_i = \tfrac{1}{2}\Big(Q_X\big(\tfrac{i}{2^k + 1}\big) + Q_X\big(\tfrac{i + 1}{2^k + 1}\big)\Big), \qquad i = 1, \dots, 2^k
$$

normalized into $$[-1, 1]$$, with an asymmetric construction ($$2^{k-1}$$ levels for negatives, $$2^{k-1} + 1$$ for positives) so that zero is represented exactly, which matters for padding and sparsity. Each block is rescaled by its absmax to $$[-1, 1]$$ and each weight snapped to the nearest $$q_i$$. Section 4 / Figure 3: NF4 beats 4-bit floats and integers on zero-shot accuracy at every model size, at the same bit budget.

**2. Double quantization.** The per-block FP32 constants cost $$32 / 64 = 0.5$$ bits per parameter. Quantize the constants themselves to 8 bits in blocks of 256 with their own FP32 constant, bringing the overhead to $$8/64 + 32/(64 \cdot 256) \approx 0.127$$ bits per parameter, about 3GB saved on a 65B model, with no loss.

**3. Paged optimizers.** Use NVIDIA unified memory to page the optimizer states to CPU RAM when a GPU spike (long sequences) would otherwise cause an out-of-memory error, and page them back when needed. This removes the crashes, not the average memory.

**Forward pass with QLoRA.** For one linear layer with 4-bit weights and LoRA adapters,

$$
Y^{\text{BF16}} = X^{\text{BF16}}\ \operatorname{doubleDequant}\big(c_1^{\text{FP32}}, c_2^{\text{k-bit}}, W^{\text{NF4}}\big) + X^{\text{BF16}} L_1^{\text{BF16}} L_2^{\text{BF16}}
$$

where $$\operatorname{doubleDequant}$$ first restores the block constants, then the weights, to BF16; the matmul runs in 16-bit and the frozen weights are re-quantized nothing. Gradients flow through the dequantized weights into $$L_1, L_2$$ only. LoRA is applied to *all* linear layers, which Section 4 finds necessary to match 16-bit full fine-tuning (attention-only LoRA was not enough at 4 bits); LoRA rank hardly matters once every layer has adapters (Figure 4 in the appendix).

## Memory (Figure 1)

Full fine-tuning of a 65B model in 16 bits needs about 780GB across GPUs (weights, gradients, Adam states); 16-bit LoRA about 160GB; QLoRA under 48GB, on one GPU. The bar chart draws the three stacks with the optimizer-state paging arrow to CPU.

## Fidelity and the Guanaco results

- **Table 3:** across LLaMA 7B–65B on 5 zero-shot tasks, NF4 + double quantization matches 16-bit LoRA and 16-bit full fine-tuning; 4-bit *without* fine-tuning loses accuracy, but QLoRA recovers it.
- **Table 4:** on GLUE (RoBERTa-large) and SuperNatural-Instructions (T5), QLoRA fully recovers the 16-bit full-finetuning performance.
- **Guanaco (Tables 5–6).** Fine-tuning LLaMA on the OASST1 dataset (9k examples) with QLoRA gives Guanaco-65B, judged by GPT-4 at 99.3% of ChatGPT's score on the Vicuna benchmark and second only to GPT-4 in an Elo tournament with human and GPT-4 judges; Guanaco-33B is competitive with Vicuna-13B while being trainable in 12 hours on one GPU. The 7B model fits on a phone-class 5GB budget.
- **Data quality vs quantity (Figure 2 / Table 6).** Fine-tuning on eight instruction datasets: the dataset matters far more than its size; OASST1 wins on chatbot quality while FLAN v2 wins on MMLU, and the two do not correlate, so "best" depends on the benchmark.
- **Evaluation study.** Human and GPT-4 judgments agree on model rankings but GPT-4 is biased toward the first response it sees and toward its own outputs; Elo with paired comparisons is more reliable than absolute scores.

## Thoughts

- NF4 is a good example of matching a code to a distribution; it works because pretrained weights are nearly Gaussian, which is not guaranteed for activations, and later work quantizes those differently.
- "LoRA on all layers" was a quiet correction to the original LoRA paper's attention-only setting and is now standard practice.
- With this paper the series ends where it can be used: a 65B model with rotary positions, SwiGLU, trained past compute-optimal, fine-tuned in 4 bits on one GPU with reasoning prompts, and served with fused attention.
