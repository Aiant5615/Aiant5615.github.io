## One-line summary

Replace TRPO's constrained optimization with a first-order objective that *clips* the probability ratio between the new and old policy, so that several epochs of minibatch gradient ascent on one batch of experience are safe. Simpler than TRPO, as stable, and better on most benchmarks.

## Why it is first in an LLM reading list

Every RLHF paper in this series ([Ziegler](/blog/ziegler-lm-preferences/), [Stiennon](/blog/stiennon-summarize/), [InstructGPT](/blog/instructgpt/)) optimizes the language model with PPO, and [GRPO](/blog/deepseekmath-grpo/) is PPO with the value network removed. Understanding the clipped objective is the prerequisite for reading any of them.

## From policy gradient to the surrogate

The vanilla policy-gradient estimator is

$$
\hat g = \hat{\mathbb{E}}_t\Big[\nabla_{\theta} \log \pi_{\theta}(a_t \mid s_t)\, \hat A_t\Big]
$$

where $$\hat A_t$$ is an estimate of the advantage (how much better action $$a_t$$ was than average in state $$s_t$$) and $$\hat{\mathbb{E}}_t$$ averages over a batch of timesteps. It is the gradient of $$L^{PG}(\theta) = \hat{\mathbb{E}}_t[\log \pi_{\theta}(a_t \mid s_t)\, \hat A_t]$$. Taking many gradient steps on the same batch with this objective is known to destroy the policy: the estimate is only valid near the policy that collected the data.

TRPO fixes this by maximizing a *surrogate* built on the probability ratio,

$$
r_t(\theta) = \frac{\pi_{\theta}(a_t \mid s_t)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a_t \mid s_t)}, \qquad \max_{\theta}\ \hat{\mathbb{E}}_t\big[r_t(\theta)\, \hat A_t\big] \ \text{ subject to } \ \hat{\mathbb{E}}_t\big[\mathrm{KL}(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}} \,\Vert\, \pi_{\theta})\big] \le \delta
$$

The ratio is 1 at the old policy, and the surrogate's gradient equals the policy gradient there; the KL constraint keeps the update in the region where the surrogate is trustworthy. TRPO needs a second-order (conjugate-gradient) solver, which is awkward with shared parameters, dropout, or large networks.

## The clipped objective

PPO keeps the ratio and drops the constraint:

$$
L^{CLIP}(\theta) = \hat{\mathbb{E}}_t\Big[\min\big(r_t(\theta)\hat A_t,\ \operatorname{clip}(r_t(\theta),\ 1 - \epsilon,\ 1 + \epsilon)\,\hat A_t\big)\Big], \qquad \epsilon = 0.2
$$

Read it per timestep. If $$\hat A_t > 0$$ (the action was good), the objective grows with $$r_t$$ until $$r_t = 1 + \epsilon$$, after which the clipped term is flat: there is *no incentive* to push the probability of that action up by more than 20%. If $$\hat A_t < 0$$ (the action was bad), the objective wants $$r_t$$ small, but the clip stops rewarding decreases below $$1 - \epsilon$$. The $$\min$$ makes the whole thing a *pessimistic lower bound* on the unclipped surrogate: clipping only ever removes improvement, never adds it, so when the ratio moves in the direction that would make the objective *worse* (an already-bad action getting more likely), the unclipped term is used and the penalty stays. Figure 1 draws exactly these two cases. Because the objective flattens instead of constraining, you can run plain SGD/Adam for $$K$$ epochs over minibatches of one batch of rollouts, and the flat regions automatically stop the policy from moving too far.

## The alternative: adaptive KL penalty

Section 4 gives the baseline PPO variant that later RLHF work borrows:

$$
L^{KLPEN}(\theta) = \hat{\mathbb{E}}_t\Big[r_t(\theta)\hat A_t - \beta\, \mathrm{KL}\big(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot \mid s_t) \,\Vert\, \pi_{\theta}(\cdot \mid s_t)\big)\Big]
$$

After each policy update compute the realized KL $$d$$; if $$d < d_{\text{targ}}/1.5$$ halve $$\beta$$, if $$d > 1.5\, d_{\text{targ}}$$ double it. The paper finds it worse than clipping in these experiments, but the *adaptive-$$\beta$$* idea is what [Ziegler et al.](/blog/ziegler-lm-preferences/) use to keep a fine-tuned language model close to its starting point.

## The full loss, and advantages

With a shared policy/value network the loss adds a value-function term and an entropy bonus,

$$
L^{CLIP+VF+S}(\theta) = \hat{\mathbb{E}}_t\Big[L^{CLIP}_t(\theta) - c_1\,\big(V_{\theta}(s_t) - V_t^{\text{targ}}\big)^2 + c_2\, S[\pi_{\theta}](s_t)\Big]
$$

and advantages come from generalized advantage estimation (GAE), a truncated, exponentially weighted sum of TD errors over a rollout of length $$T$$:

$$
\hat A_t = \delta_t + (\gamma\lambda)\delta_{t+1} + \cdots + (\gamma\lambda)^{T-t+1}\delta_{T-1}, \qquad \delta_t = r_t + \gamma V(s_{t+1}) - V(s_t)
$$

$$\lambda$$ trades bias against variance ($$\lambda = 1$$ gives the Monte-Carlo return minus baseline; $$\lambda = 0$$ gives one-step TD). The algorithm (Algorithm 1): $$N$$ parallel actors each collect $$T$$ steps with $$\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$$, compute $$\hat A$$, then optimize the loss for $$K$$ epochs with minibatches of size $$M \le NT$$, then repeat. Typical MuJoCo settings: $$T = 2048$$, $$K = 10$$, $$M = 64$$, $$\gamma = 0.99$$, $$\lambda = 0.95$$, Adam at $$3 \times 10^{-4}$$.

## Results

New state of the art or competitive on the continuous-control suite, with a far simpler implementation than TRPO; Atari results better than A2C and comparable to ACER, which is more complex. The paper became the default policy-gradient method within months.

## Thoughts

- In RLHF the "environment" is a prompt, the "action" is a whole response (or each token), and the reward is a learned model's scalar. Everything above transfers unchanged, which is why PPO was the natural choice.
- The KL-to-old-policy inside PPO and the KL-to-*reference*-policy that RLHF adds are different things: the first controls the step size per iteration, the second keeps the final model near the SFT model. Both appear in later papers and are easy to confuse.
- GRPO drops $$V_{\theta}$$ and replaces GAE with group-normalized rewards; when the value network is as large as the policy (as for LLMs), that halves the memory.
