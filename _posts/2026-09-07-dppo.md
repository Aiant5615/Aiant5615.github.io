## One-line summary

Take a Diffusion Policy pre-trained by behavior cloning, unroll its $$K$$ denoising steps inside every environment step to get a "Diffusion Policy MDP", and run [PPO](/blog/ppo/) on the Gaussian likelihood of each denoising step. The conjecture in earlier work was that policy gradients would be hopeless here because the effective horizon is $$K$$ times longer; DPPO shows the opposite. With a few design rules (fine-tune only the last denoising steps, or a short DDIM chain; discount across denoising steps; floor the noise) it beats off-policy Q-learning, weighted regression, and Gaussian/GMM policies with PPO on Gym, Franka Kitchen, Robomimic and Furniture-Bench, and transfers zero-shot to a real robot.

## Why it matters

[DDPO](/blog/ddpo/) treats one image generation as an episode. Robotics has an *outer* MDP, the environment, and the diffusion model is the policy inside it. DPPO is the paper that writes down the composite MDP and works out what advantage estimate, value function and sampler make on-policy fine-tuning of a diffusion policy stable. It also gives the first careful argument for *why* diffusion policies plus policy gradients work well: exploration stays on the data manifold and the iterative refinement is preserved.

## Background: Diffusion Policy

A DDPM represents $$p(x^0)$$ as the reverse of a noising process. With noise predictor $$\varepsilon_{\theta}(x^k, k)$$, sampling runs $$k = K, \dots, 1$$:

$$
x^{k-1} \sim p_{\theta}(x^{k-1} \mid x^k) := \mathcal{N}\big(x^{k-1};\ \mu_k(x^k, \varepsilon_{\theta}(x^k, k)),\ \sigma_k^2 I\big)
$$

where $$\mu_k$$ is a fixed function and $$\sigma_k$$ follows a fixed schedule. Diffusion Policy conditions the noise predictor on the state, $$\varepsilon_{\theta}(a^k, s, k)$$, and predicts an **action chunk** of $$T_p$$ future steps, of which $$T_a \le T_p$$ are executed before re-planning; throughout, $$a$$ means the executed chunk. Crucially the policy has no explicit density $$p_{\theta}(a^0 \mid s)$$, which is what blocks a naive policy gradient.

## The two-layer Diffusion Policy MDP (Section 4.1, Figure 3)

Environment MDP $$\mathcal{M}_{\text{ENV}} = (\mathcal{S}, \mathcal{A}, P_0, P, R)$$ at time $$t$$; denoising index $$k$$ counts down from $$K-1$$ to $$0$$. Define the flattened time $$\bar{t}(t, k) = tK + (K - k - 1)$$, which increases with $$t$$ and, within a step, as $$k$$ decreases. Then

$$
\bar{s}_{\bar{t}(t,k)} = (s_t, a_t^{k+1}), \qquad \bar{a}_{\bar{t}(t,k)} = a_t^{k}, \qquad \bar{R}_{\bar{t}(t,k)} = \begin{cases} 0 & k > 0 \\ R(s_t, a_t^0) & k = 0 \end{cases}
$$

The state is the environment state together with the *current noisy action*; the action is the next, slightly-less-noisy action; reward is paid only when the fully denoised $$a_t^0$$ is executed. Initial distribution $$\bar{P}_0 = P_0 \otimes \mathcal{N}(0, I)$$ (environment start plus pure-noise action). Transitions: for $$k > 0$$ move deterministically to $$(s_t, a_t^k)$$; for $$k = 0$$ step the environment $$s_{t+1} \sim P(\cdot \mid s_t, a_t^0)$$ and draw fresh noise $$a_{t+1}^K \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$$, so the pure-noise action is treated as part of the environment. The policy in this MDP is the denoising step itself,

$$
\bar{\pi}_{\theta}(\bar{a} \mid \bar{s}) = \pi_{\theta}(a_t^k \mid a_t^{k+1}, s_t) = \mathcal{N}\big(a_t^k;\ \mu(a_t^{k+1}, \varepsilon_{\theta}(a_t^{k+1}, k+1, s_t)),\ \sigma_{k+1}^2 I\big)
$$

a Gaussian whose log-density is analytic. So the vanilla policy gradient applies:

$$
\nabla_{\theta} \bar{J}(\bar{\pi}_{\theta}) = \mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{\bar{t} \ge 0} \nabla_{\theta} \log \bar{\pi}_{\theta}(\bar{a}_{\bar{t}} \mid \bar{s}_{\bar{t}})\ \bar{r}(\bar{s}_{\bar{t}}, \bar{a}_{\bar{t}})\Big], \qquad \bar{r}(\bar{s}_{\bar{t}}, \bar{a}_{\bar{t}}) := \sum_{\tau \ge \bar{t}} \gamma(\tau)\, \bar{R}(\bar{s}_{\tau}, \bar{a}_{\tau})
$$

Sampling a trajectory of this MDP is exactly the ordinary Diffusion Policy rollout, so nothing new has to be simulated.

## Instantiating PPO (Section 4.2)

The clipped PPO update in the composite MDP is

$$
\nabla_{\theta}\, \mathbb{E}_{(\bar{s}, \bar{a}) \sim \bar{\pi}_{\theta_{\text{old}}}} \min\Big(\hat{A}\, \frac{\bar{\pi}_{\theta}(\bar{a} \mid \bar{s})}{\bar{\pi}_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\bar{a} \mid \bar{s})},\ \hat{A}\, \text{clip}\Big(\frac{\bar{\pi}_{\theta}(\bar{a} \mid \bar{s})}{\bar{\pi}_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\bar{a} \mid \bar{s})}, 1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon\Big)\Big)
$$

What is specific to DPPO is the advantage. Since rewards arrive only at $$k = 0$$, the return from any denoising step equals the environment-discounted return from the executed action:

$$
\bar{r}(\bar{s}_{\bar{t}}, \bar{a}_{\bar{t}}) = \sum_{t' \ge t} \gamma_{\text{ENV}}^{t'}\, \bar{r}\big(\bar{s}_{\bar{t}(t', 0)}, \bar{a}_{\bar{t}(t', 0)}\big)
$$

This means no value needs to be learned at intermediate $$k$$. The advantage is then **discounted across denoising steps** with a second factor $$\gamma_{\text{DENOISE}}$$:

$$
\hat{A}(\bar{s}_{\bar{t}}, \bar{a}_{\bar{t}}) := \gamma_{\text{DENOISE}}^{\,k}\Big(\bar{r}(\bar{s}_{\bar{t}}, \bar{a}_{\bar{t}}) - \hat{V}(\bar{s}_{\bar{t}(t,0)})\Big), \qquad \hat{V}(\bar{s}_{\bar{t}(t,0)}) := \tilde{V}(s_t)
$$

Noisier steps (large $$k$$) get less credit, which the ablations show matters, and the critic is a function of the environment state only, which trains more stably than a critic that also sees the nearly-denoised action. In practice GAE replaces the plain return.

## Best practices (Section 4.3)

- **Fine-tune only the last $$K'$$ denoising steps.** Keep a frozen copy $$\theta$$ for early steps and update a copy $$\theta_{\text{FT}}$$ used for the last few. Faster, less memory, same final performance. Diffusion Policy often uses $$K = 100$$.
- **Or fine-tune a DDIM sampler.** DDIM with $$K_{\text{DDIM}} \ll K$$ (as few as 5 steps):

$$
x^{k-1} \sim p_{\theta}^{\text{DDIM}}(x^{k-1} \mid x^k) := \mathcal{N}\big(x^{k-1};\ \mu^{\text{DDIM}}(x^k, \varepsilon_{\theta}(x^k, k)),\ \eta\, \sigma_k^2 I\big)
$$

DDIM is usually deterministic ($$\eta = 0$$), which would make the likelihood a Dirac delta, so training uses $$\eta = 1$$ (exploration noise) and evaluation uses $$\eta = 0$$. Used for pixel-based and furniture tasks.
- **Noise floors.** The cosine schedule anneals $$\sigma_k$$ to about $$10^{-4}$$ at $$k = 0$$, which would kill exploration; clip it to $$\sigma^{\text{exp}}_{\min} \in [0.01, 0.1]$$ when sampling, and to at least $$\sigma^{\text{prob}}_{\min} = 0.1$$ when evaluating the log-likelihood so its magnitude stays bounded.
- **Architecture.** MLP heads fine-tune most stably; UNet heads allow $$T_a < T_p$$ (pre-train with long chunks, fine-tune executing shorter ones).

## Results (Section 5)

- **vs diffusion-based RL (Figure 5).** Against DRWR/DAWR (reward- and advantage-weighted regression, the authors' own baselines), DIPO, IDQL, DQL and QSM on Gym (Hopper, Walker2D, HalfCheetah, D4RL medium data) and Robomimic (Lift, Can, Square, Transport, multi-human data, sparse reward). DPPO is consistent and stable; the Q-learning methods are competitive on Gym but collapse or stall on Transport, which DPPO solves.
- **vs demo-augmented RL (Figure 6)** on HalfCheetah, Franka Kitchen and Robomimic: stronger final performance in the long-horizon, high-dimensional manipulation tasks.
- **vs other parameterizations (Figure 7).** Gaussian and GMM policies (MLP or Transformer) with PPO, state and pixel inputs. DPPO reaches near-100% on Lift/Can and above 90% on Transport with state; with pixels it is the first RL method reported to bring Transport above 50%, while the Gaussian policy stays at its 0% pre-trained rate.
- **Furniture-Bench and hardware (Figures 8–9).** One-leg, Lamp and Round-table with Low/Med randomization; Gaussian-MLP collapses on most, DPPO improves on all six. The One-leg policy trained in simulation is deployed zero-shot on hardware over 20 trials, with a much smaller sim-to-real gap than the baseline; fine-tuning teaches corrective behaviors such as re-grasping after a failed insertion.

## Why it works (Section 6, Figures 10–12)

Using the 2-D Avoid task from D3IL: DPPO's exploration during fine-tuning stays in the multi-modal "manifold" of pre-training trajectories, while Gaussian and GMM policies explore by adding isotropic noise around a mean. Injected action noise and perturbed dynamics hurt DPPO less (Figure 11). Figure 12 shows the action distribution across denoising steps stays iterative and multi-modal through fine-tuning instead of collapsing to a point.

## Thoughts

- The formal move is small (chain [DDPO](/blog/ddpo/)'s MDPs along time) but the engineering findings are the real content: value on $$s_t$$ only, denoising discount, and the $$\eta = 1$$ DDIM trick that turns a deterministic sampler back into a policy with a density.
- The paper's own reading of *why* it works, exploration along the data manifold, is the strongest argument I know for using expressive generative policies with on-policy RL rather than Q-learning on top of them.
- The cost is that every environment step becomes $$K'$$ policy steps of credit assignment, which is why the method leans on short DDIM chains. The next paper faces the same problem for flow models, where there is no stochastic sampler to begin with.
