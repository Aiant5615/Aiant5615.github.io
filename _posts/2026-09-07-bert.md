## One-line summary

Pretrain a Transformer *encoder* so that every token can attend to both its left and right context, using two self-supervised tasks (predict masked-out words; predict whether two sentences are adjacent), then fine-tune the same network with one extra output layer per task. Eleven NLP benchmarks improve at once, several by a large margin.

## Why it matters

[GPT](/blog/gpt1/) is left-to-right by construction, so at any token it cannot use what comes later. ELMo was bidirectional but only by concatenating two separate one-directional LSTMs. BERT's masked objective is what makes a *deep, jointly* bidirectional Transformer trainable, and its fine-tuning results made "pretrained encoder" the default for classification, tagging and extractive QA.

## Why not just train bidirectionally?

A standard LM predicts token $$t$$ from tokens $$< t$$. If attention is bidirectional, the model can trivially "see itself" through the stack: the representation of position $$t$$ at layer 2 can copy token $$t$$ from layer 1, and the prediction becomes a lookup. Hiding the token is the fix.

## Masked language modeling (MLM)

Choose 15% of the input positions at random. For each chosen position, the model must predict the original token from the final hidden vector at that position through a softmax over the vocabulary (cross-entropy on the chosen positions only). Instead of always replacing with `[MASK]`:

- 80% of the time replace with `[MASK]`,
- 10% of the time replace with a random token,
- 10% of the time keep the original token.

The reason: `[MASK]` never appears at fine-tuning time, so a model that only ever sees `[MASK]` at predicted positions would learn representations that are calibrated for a token that later never occurs. Mixing in random and unchanged tokens forces every position's representation to stay informative about the true token. Appendix C.2 ablates the mixture; the 80/10/10 split is best or tied on all tasks. Because only 15% of positions produce a training signal, MLM converges more slowly per step than a left-to-right LM, but Figure 5 (appendix) shows it overtakes almost immediately in accuracy.

## Next-sentence prediction (NSP)

Sample pairs of segments: 50% of the time B actually follows A in the corpus (label IsNext), 50% of the time B is a random segment (NotNext). The final vector of the first token `[CLS]`, $$C \in \mathbb{R}^H$$, is passed through a linear layer and softmax to predict the label. The intent is to teach the model about relationships between sentences, which QA and NLI need. (Later work, RoBERTa, found NSP unnecessary once documents are used as long contiguous inputs; the paper's own ablation in Table 5 shows a modest gain on QNLI, MNLI and SQuAD.)

## Input representation

A single sequence handles both single sentences and pairs: `[CLS] tokens of A [SEP] tokens of B [SEP]`. Each input vector is the **sum** of three embeddings,

$$
E_i = E^{\text{token}}_{w_i} + E^{\text{segment}}_{s_i} + E^{\text{position}}_{i}
$$

where the segment embedding is one of two learned vectors (A or B) and the position embedding is learned, up to length 512. WordPiece vocabulary of 30,000. Sizes: BERT-base $$L = 12$$ layers, $$H = 768$$, $$A = 12$$ heads (110M parameters, chosen to match GPT); BERT-large $$L = 24$$, $$H = 1024$$, $$A = 16$$ (340M). Pretraining data: BooksCorpus (800M words) + English Wikipedia (2,500M words), 1M steps at batch size 256 sequences × 512 tokens, about 40 epochs, on 16 (base) or 64 (large) Cloud TPUs for four days.

## Fine-tuning

Feed the task input in the same format and add one output layer:

- **Sentence-level tasks** (GLUE): classification from $$C$$ with a new weight matrix $$W \in \mathbb{R}^{K \times H}$$, $$\log \operatorname{softmax}(C W^{\top})$$.
- **Token-level tasks** (NER): a per-token classifier on each final vector $$T_i$$.
- **Extractive QA** (SQuAD): learn a start vector $$S \in \mathbb{R}^H$$ and an end vector $$E \in \mathbb{R}^H$$. The probability that token $$i$$ starts the answer is

$$
P_i = \frac{e^{S \cdot T_i}}{\sum_j e^{S \cdot T_j}}
$$

and similarly for the end with $$E$$. The score of a span from $$i$$ to $$j$$ is $$S \cdot T_i + E \cdot T_j$$, maximized over $$j \ge i$$; training maximizes the log-likelihood of the correct start and end. For SQuAD 2.0, "no answer" is a span on the `[CLS]` token, and a threshold $$\tau$$ on $$\hat s_{ij} - s_{\text{null}}$$ decides whether to answer.

All parameters are fine-tuned, typically for 2–4 epochs with a small learning rate ($$2 \times 10^{-5}$$ to $$5 \times 10^{-5}$$) and batch size 16–32; each task takes at most an hour on a TPU.

## Results

GLUE test score **80.5** for BERT-large (GPT: 72.8), with MNLI 86.7. SQuAD v1.1 F1 **93.2** (single model 91.0), above the human 91.2; SQuAD v2.0 F1 83.1 (+5.1 over the prior best). SWAG 86.3 (+27 absolute over the previous best). Ablations (Table 5): removing NSP costs a little; a left-to-right model without NSP costs a lot (SQuAD F1 drops ~8, even with a BiLSTM on top). Table 6: bigger is better on every task, even the tiny ones. Table 7: features extracted from BERT without fine-tuning also work well for NER (concatenating the last four layers is within 0.3 F1 of fine-tuning).

## Thoughts

- The two OpenAI/Google papers of 2018 are the same Transformer with the mask removed or kept. Removing it wins on understanding benchmarks; keeping it makes generation possible. [T5](/blog/t5/) tries to have both.
- The 80/10/10 rule is a small piece of engineering that fixes a train/test mismatch; most "why does BERT work" questions come back to the masking design.
- The span-scoring formula is worth memorizing: two dot products and an argmax over $$j \ge i$$ is still how extractive QA is done.
