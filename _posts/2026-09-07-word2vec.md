## One-line summary

Drop the nonlinear hidden layer from the neural language model so that word vectors can be trained on billions of words in a day, and show that the resulting vectors encode linear relationships between words.

## Why it matters

[Bengio's model](/blog/2026/09/07/nplm-bengio/) learned good embeddings but was too slow to train on large corpora. Mikolov et al. asked a different question: if all we want is the embedding matrix, what is the cheapest model whose training still forces the vectors to be useful? The answer, two log-linear models called **CBOW** and **Skip-gram**, produced vectors on 1.6 billion words and became the default input representation for NLP until contextual models ([BERT](/blog/2026/09/07/bert/)) replaced them.

## Training cost as the design principle

The paper frames every architecture by its training complexity,

$$
O = E \times T \times Q
$$

where $$E$$ is the number of epochs, $$T$$ the number of training words, and $$Q$$ the cost per word. For the feed-forward NNLM with $$N$$ context words, embedding size $$D$$, hidden size $$H$$ and vocabulary $$V$$,

$$
Q_{\text{NNLM}} = N \times D + N \times D \times H + H \times V
$$

The three terms are the projection, the hidden layer, and the output softmax. The last term dominates ($$V$$ is huge), but with a **hierarchical softmax** over a Huffman-coded binary tree it drops to $$H \times \log_2 V$$, and then the hidden layer $$N \times D \times H$$ becomes the bottleneck. For the recurrent NNLM the cost is $$Q_{\text{RNNLM}} = H \times H + H \times V$$, with the same fix for the second term.

The two new models remove the hidden layer entirely.

**CBOW (continuous bag of words).** Average the vectors of the surrounding words and predict the middle word from the average:

$$
Q_{\text{CBOW}} = N \times D + D \times \log_2 V
$$

"Bag of words" because the average ignores word order. The paper uses four words before and four after.

**Skip-gram.** Do the reverse: from the middle word, predict each of the $$C$$ surrounding words separately, sampling nearer words more often:

$$
Q_{\text{Skip-gram}} = C \times \big(D + D \times \log_2 V\big)
$$

Skip-gram is slower per word than CBOW but produces better semantic vectors, especially for rare words, because each occurrence generates $$C$$ training pairs.

**What is being maximized.** Both models maximize the average log-probability of the target word given the context under a softmax over vectors; for Skip-gram,

$$
\frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^{T} \sum_{-c \le j \le c,\ j \ne 0} \log p(w_{t+j} \mid w_t), \qquad p(w_O \mid w_I) = \frac{\exp(v'^{\top}_{w_O} v_{w_I})}{\sum_{w=1}^{V} \exp(v'^{\top}_{w} v_{w_I})}
$$

with an *input* vector $$v$$ and an *output* vector $$v'$$ per word. This full softmax is never computed; the ICLR paper uses the hierarchical softmax, and the NeurIPS follow-up ("Distributed Representations of Words and Phrases", also 2013) introduces **negative sampling**, which replaces it with $$\log \sigma(v'^{\top}_{w_O} v_{w_I}) + \sum_{i=1}^{k} \mathbb{E}_{w_i \sim P_n(w)} \big[\log \sigma(-v'^{\top}_{w_i} v_{w_I})\big]$$: pull the true context word up, push $$k$$ random words down.

## The analogy test

The evaluation is the memorable part. If vectors encode relations linearly, then the vector arithmetic

$$
X = \text{vector}(\text{"biggest"}) - \text{vector}(\text{"big"}) + \text{vector}(\text{"small"})
$$

should land near "smallest". The paper builds 8,869 semantic questions (capital–country, currency, city–state, man–woman) and 10,675 syntactic questions (adjective–adverb, comparative, superlative, verb tenses, plurals) and counts a question as correct only when the nearest vector by cosine similarity, excluding the three query words, is exactly the expected word.

## Figures and tables, explained

- **Figure 1 (the two architectures).** Left, CBOW: several input word positions feed into one *projection* layer (their vectors are summed), which feeds one output. Right, Skip-gram: one input word, one projection, several outputs. There is no hidden layer box in either; the projection *is* the embedding.
- **Table 3 (architecture comparison).** RNNLM and NNLM vectors score in the low tens of percent; CBOW and Skip-gram reach 24% and 55% on semantic questions and 64% and 59% on syntactic ones, trained on the same 320M words with $$D = 640$$.
- **Table 4 / Figure 2 (more data vs more dimensions).** Accuracy grows when both the corpus and the dimensionality grow together; increasing one alone saturates.
- **Table 5 (training time).** The point of the paper in one row: Skip-gram on 783M words in a day on a single machine, versus NNLM needing weeks on a cluster.
- **Table 8 (example relations).** "Paris − France + Italy ≈ Rome", "Einstein − scientist + Messi ≈ midfielder", plus failures, which the authors show honestly.

## Results

Skip-gram with 1000 dimensions on 6B words reached 65.6% semantic and 66.1% syntactic accuracy on their test set and set new state of the art on the Microsoft sentence completion challenge when combined with an RNNLM.

## Thoughts

- The design lesson is "make the model cheaper so you can use more data", the same trade the Transformer made against RNNs and GPT-3 made against everything.
- The linear structure ("king − man + woman") was later explained partly as a consequence of the factorized PMI matrix the model implicitly approximates (Levy & Goldberg, 2014).
- Static vectors cannot separate "bank (river)" from "bank (money)". That limitation is what contextual embeddings from [ELMo/BERT](/blog/2026/09/07/bert/) fix.
