## One-line summary

Collect 130,000 teleoperated episodes covering more than 700 language instructions on 13 robots over 17 months, and train one Transformer that maps a short history of camera images plus the instruction to discretized robot actions at 3 Hz. The model absorbs the diverse data without losing per-task performance, generalizes to unseen instructions, objects and backgrounds far better than prior imitation models, and even benefits from simulated and other-robot data mixed in.

## Why it opens the VLA list

RT-1 establishes the recipe that every later vision-language-action model keeps: actions as *tokens* predicted by a Transformer, a language instruction as conditioning, a large multi-task real-robot dataset, and evaluation by generalization axes (unseen tasks, distractors, backgrounds, long-horizon). RT-2 replaces the vision backbone with a web-scale VLM; Octo and OpenVLA scale the dataset across robots; π0 changes the action head.

## Architecture (Figure 3)

**Inputs.** A history of 6 images (300×300) from the robot's head camera and a natural-language instruction.

**Instruction embedding.** The instruction is encoded with the Universal Sentence Encoder (USE) into a 512-d vector.

**FiLM-conditioned image encoder.** Each image goes through an ImageNet-pretrained EfficientNet-B3; the instruction embedding is injected with **FiLM** layers (feature-wise affine modulation) at multiple depths, so the visual features are computed *for* the instruction from the start ("early fusion") rather than concatenated afterwards. Output: a 9×9×512 feature map, i.e. 81 visual tokens per image. FiLM layers are initialized to the identity so the pretrained EfficientNet is not disturbed early in training.

**TokenLearner.** An attention-based module selects a soft combination of the 81 tokens into **8 tokens** per image; with 6 images the Transformer sees 48 tokens instead of 486, which is what makes real-time inference possible.

**Transformer.** A decoder-only Transformer (8 self-attention layers, 19M parameters) over the 48 tokens, outputting **action tokens**.

**Action tokenization.** An action has 11 dimensions: 7 for the arm ($$x, y, z$$, roll, pitch, yaw, gripper opening), 3 for the mobile base ($$x, y$$, yaw) and 1 discrete mode (arm / base / terminate). Each continuous dimension is discretized uniformly into **256 bins**, and the model is trained with **cross-entropy** over the bins per dimension. Only the current action is predicted (no action chunking); the whole policy runs at 3 Hz on the robot with a 100 ms budget.

**Loss.** Standard categorical cross-entropy on the 11 action tokens given the observation history and instruction; behaviour cloning, no reward.

## Data (Section 5.2)

Everyday Robots mobile manipulators in "robot classrooms" (office-kitchen mock-ups) and two real kitchens (Figure 2); skills: pick, place, open/close drawers, knock over, upright, move near, place upright, open jars, pick-from-drawer; 744 instructions; 130k episodes, collected by teleoperation with VR controllers, each annotated with an instruction. Data is deliberately balanced over skills and objects.

## Evaluation and results (Section 6)

Over 3,000 real-robot trials. Baselines: Gato (a decoder-only Transformer with all image tokens) and BC-Z (a ResNet policy), trained on the same data.

- **Seen tasks:** RT-1 97% vs 65% (Gato) and 72% (BC-Z).
- **Unseen instructions** (new combinations of skills and objects): 76%, +25 points over the best baseline.
- **Distractors** (Figure 4, top): 83% averaged over easy/medium/hard scenes; **backgrounds** (new kitchen counters, lighting): 59%; both far above baselines.
- **Long-horizon (SayCan):** used as the low-level policy under a language planner for up to 50 steps, RT-1 executes 67% of long-horizon instructions in the harder Kitchen2, vs 33% for BC-Z.
- **Heterogeneous data (Table 5, Figure 6):** adding simulation episodes improves real-world success on sim-only objects (from 23% to 87%) without hurting real tasks; adding data from a *different* robot (Kuka bin-picking) raises success on bin-picking on the Everyday robot from 22% to 39%.
- **Ablations (Table 4):** removing the Transformer (using an MLP) or the pretrained EfficientNet costs the most on generalization; removing history and using autoregressive action decoding cost a little; discretization vs continuous actions matters less.
- **Data ablations (Table 6):** more *tasks* matters more than more *episodes per task*; cutting task diversity by 30% drops generalization by 20+ points.

## Thoughts

- The action-as-token choice is the one that carries forward; the 256-bin uniform discretization survives unchanged in RT-2 and OpenVLA.
- Early fusion of language into vision (FiLM) is replaced in later models by simply putting image and text tokens into the same Transformer, which is simpler and lets pretraining carry over.
- The strongest finding is about data: diversity of tasks, not episode count, drives generalization, which is the argument behind cross-robot datasets like Open X-Embodiment.
